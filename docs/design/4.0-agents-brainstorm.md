## 1. Who the agents are

### Agents versus things

- An **agent** is anything that **makes choices**: a person, a family, a town, a lord, a guild.
- **Things** don't choose; they follow rules: land, rivers, forests, animals (mostly), goods, **buildings** (houses, fields, markets).
- **The two are separate systems.** Agents *own*, *use* and *build* things; a family lives in a house, but the house is an object in the world, not part of the family. *(Owner's point, 20 Nov.)*

### The cast

From smallest to largest. Agreed in outline; **each one gets expanded in detail later.**

| # | Agent | Group or individual | Chooses… | Borrowed from |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Person**: a name, a race, an age, parents, skills | Individual (stored as data) | — (mostly decided for by their family) | Dwarf Fortress' historical figures; Crusader Kings' family trees |

### Ideas agreed or raised

- **Families and family trees** *(owner, 20 Nov)*: people have **names** and a **race**; families share a surname and have a **family tree**, stored as data. **Children are born and grow up.** A village might hold about ten families.
- **A worked example to keep in mind** *(owner)*: a family of three. The father is a small farmer with a few fields by his cabin; the mother cooks and keeps the house, and sells their goods at the nearby market; the child goes to school if there is one, otherwise helps with chores and the father's work. **As realistic as what people really did.** *(Real history, approximate: see section 1a.)*
- **Who decides for a settlement:** a **village is run by its lord** (or by village elders for small things); a **town can buy a charter** and run itself through a council. Agreed: realistic.
- **Races organise themselves differently**, agreed: **dwarves in clans** under a hold, **elves under a council of elders** (the same elves rule for centuries), **humans in feudal** lords and vassals. Same code, different "who decides" rules per race.
- **Religion as its own layer** *(owner)*, like the magic layer: religions **develop and spread**, shaped by race and environment, with followers, temples and monasteries. Needs research into what religion actually changed (first ideas in section 1b).
- **Bandits come out of the simulation** *(owner)*: when a village is hit by a disaster and people have to leave, some become bandits; children can be born into bandit families. Bandits rob caravans, so **caravans need guards**, which is a new job.
- **Services are goods too** *(owner)*: the economy needs people buying and selling not only items but **work**: guards, soldiers, bakers, market stallholders.
- **Fantasy races beyond the three:** **no orcs** (the owner doesn't like them). **Monsters** as a kind of creature changed by magic, as in *Mushoku Tensei* and *That Time I Got Reincarnated as a Slime*. More in section 1c.

### Decided after the first round *(20 Nov)*

- **The family is the deciding agent**, for now. Every person is stored with a name, race, birth year, parents and skills (cheap, so everyone has a family tree), but **only the family decides**. Pops are just totals added up from the families.

  | Level | Stored how | Cost |
  |---|---|---|
  | Every person | individual record: name, race, born, mother, father, skills | cheap: ~150 MB for a million people |
  | Every family / household | individual record; **makes the decisions** about once a month | moderate: ~200,000 families per million people |
  | Pops | not stored: added up from families when needed | free |

- **Marriages are simulated.** Families join when children marry; a bride or groom may move to another village, which links villages together.
  - **Royal and noble weddings** connect faraway capitals and help trade between them, as in Crusader Kings.
  - We'll be able to draw a **map of the relationships** between towns and capitals.
  - **Trade caravans also carry delegates** and messages for politics.
- **Races: only humans, elves and dwarves at first.** Later, maybe: **merfolk**, **gnomes**, **giants** and **tree people** (the owner loves Tolkien's Ents; ours would be our own tree-folk, not copies). **No lizardfolk, no orcs.**
- **Bandits can return to normal life:** a band settles and founds a village, or a lord pardons and hires them. That's a **big event**, and the leader becomes a notable, named person. Hard for the AI to decide well, so it needs careful rules.
- **Agents founding and growing villages** is one of the owner's favourite parts of the whole idea.
- **Apprenticeship** (about age 12–14, about 7 years) is how children move from villages to towns and learn trades: a flow of people and skills.

### 1a. What real medieval families did *(general history knowledge, approximate)*

- **Household size:** a peasant household was usually **4–6 people**; many children died young.
- **Children:**
  - from about **5–7** they helped: minding younger children, herding geese and pigs, scaring birds off the crops, gathering firewood, fetching water;
  - from about **12–14** they did nearly adult work, or were sent away as **apprentices** (often for about 7 years) or **servants** in another household;
  - **schools were rare**: parish, cathedral and monastery schools, mostly for boys heading for the Church or trade. Most peasants couldn't read.
- **Women's work on a farm:** dairy (milk, butter, cheese), poultry and eggs, the vegetable garden, **brewing ale** (village "alewives" sold it), spinning and weaving, cooking, and helping in the fields at harvest. They often took the surplus to market.
- **A small farmer** typically held a strip of land in the village's **open fields** rather than fields by his own cabin; the village decided together which field lay fallow. Lonely farmsteads existed too, especially in hilly or forest land.

### 1b. What religion could change *(first ideas, to research)*

- **Holy days:** fewer working days.
- **Fasting rules:** Christian Lent and fish days created enormous demand for **fish**, which drove the medieval herring trade. A religion's food rules change demand.
- **Tithes:** about a tenth of produce paid to the Church.
- **Pilgrimages:** travel, inns along pilgrim roads, a trade in relics and souvenirs.
- **Monasteries:** clearing land, farming, learning, charity in famines.
- **Legitimacy:** rulers crowned by priests; religion decides who may rule.
- **Taboos on goods:** forbidden foods or materials.
- **Shaped by the land:** sea gods on coasts, harvest gods among farmers, mountain or stone gods among dwarves.

### 1c. Other races and monsters *(suggestions, to decide)*

- Possible races: merfolk (sea trade, island peoples), beastfolk (as in *Mushoku Tensei*), gnomes (tinkerers), giants, lizardfolk in the marshes.
- **Monsters made by magic** *(owner's idea)*: in *Mushoku Tensei*, monsters carry **magic stones**, which are valuable. That's an economic hook: monster parts as magic reagents, hunters and an adventurers' guild who sell them.

## 2. What each agent strives for, and how it decides

### What they want: a ladder of needs *(agreed for now, may be developed later)*

A family looks after the **lowest unmet need first**, like Victoria 3's basic, everyday and luxury needs, or RimWorld's needs.

| Rung | Need | Met by |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Food** | grain, bread, fish, meat, ale |
| 2 | **Warmth and shelter** | firewood, clothing, a house in repair |
| 3 | **Safety** | a lord's protection, walls, not being robbed |
| 4 | **Comfort** | better food, tools, pots, furniture, salt, spices |
| 5 | **Standing and the future** | savings, land, a good marriage, apprenticing the children, faith |

The ladder is also the source of **demand**: rungs 1–2 are used up all the time, which gives the steady demand VISION asks for.

### What each agent strives for

| Agent | Strives for |
|---|---|
| Family | the needs ladder |
| Trader | profit per day of travel, without getting robbed |
| Settlement | everyone fed (a full granary), growth, having what a place its size should have (market, tavern, walls) |
| Lord | income, power, prestige, a strong family line, loyalty (or not) to their overlord |
| Faction / kingdom | security, size, wealth |
| Guild | its members' incomes and standards |
| Monastery / temple | faith, land, learning, charity |
| Bandit | loot without getting caught, and perhaps a way back |

### How they decide: scoring the options *(agreed)*

Three common methods:
1. **Fixed rules** ("if food < 1 month, buy grain"): fast but stiff.
2. **Scoring every option** (*utility AI*, as in The Sims): list the options, score each from the agent's needs, pick the best. Flexible, cheap, explainable. **← chosen**
3. **Planning chains of actions** (goal planners): clever but too slow for 200,000 families; maybe for a few notable people.

Two touches on top of scoring:
- **Personality changes the scores** (traits like *greedy*, *cautious*, *ambitious*, *pious*, as in Crusader Kings and RimWorld): a few multiplying numbers.
- **Habit:** a family only changes its life (job, home) when the new option beats the old one by a clear margin. Realistic, and it stops the world flipping back and forth (a Python-prototype problem).

**Worked example: a trader in Riverford chooses a run.** Score = profit per day × (1 − danger weight × danger).

| Option | Profit | Days | Per day | Danger | Cautious (weight 2) | Greedy (weight 1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Salt to Hillcross | 60 | 4 | 15 | 0.1 | 15 × 0.8 = **12** | 15 × 0.9 = 13.5 |
| Iron to Deepdelve | 120 | 6 | 20 | 0.3 | 20 × 0.4 = 8 | 20 × 0.7 = **14** |

The cautious trader takes the salt; the greedy one takes the iron. Same world, different people, different stories.

### Decided in the second round *(20 Nov)*

- **Inheritance: yes**, with rules that **differ by race** (and perhaps by kingdom). The two historical systems:
  - **split between the children** (*partible*): farms shrink each generation until younger sons leave to clear new land;
  - **eldest takes all** (*primogeniture*): younger children become soldiers, priests, merchants, apprentices or colonists.
  - Either way, **landless younger children are the ones who found new villages.**
- **Serfdom: not for now.** This is a fantasy world and shouldn't be *too* realistic. Kept as an idea for a future "super-realistic" mode. *(The history, for later: serfs were tied to their lord's land; after the Black Death they could demand freedom or flee to towns, where "town air makes you free" after a year and a day.)*
- **Every family has a personality**, and **children inherit traits** from their parents (with some variation). Races have tendencies too (dwarves, elves and humans differ), but every family and person is still different.
- **Limited knowledge: yes.** Agents only know news that has reached them, so traders have to **travel to places to get information**. Each trader keeps a small notebook of prices and how old they are.
- **Banks** *(owner's idea)*: add them. See section 2a.
- **Habit: yes.** People in the past didn't change their lives much.
- **What matters most to the owner:** that walking into a town feels like a **thriving, lively world**, with people about doing things and lots of goods being traded.
- **The owner's big idea: neural networks** as an optional second "mode" of decision-making alongside scoring, maybe with a local AI looking over decisions now and then, and memories. Different networks per kind of agent (a family's differs from a trader's). Research and discussion in section 2b.

### 2a. Banks *(owner's idea; history from general knowledge, approximate)*

- Medieval banking grew up in **Italian cities** (Florence, Venice, Genoa) from the 1200s; the **Medici** bank later had branches across Europe.
- **Moneychangers** sat at benches (*banco*, hence "bank") swapping coins from different places.
- **Bills of exchange:** a merchant paid money in Florence and got a paper he could cash in Bruges, so he didn't have to carry coin past bandits. That's a great fit for us: banks make long-distance trade **safer**.
- The **Knights Templar** did something similar for pilgrims travelling to Jerusalem.
- **The Church banned charging interest** (*usury*), so bankers hid interest inside exchange rates and fees; some groups were allowed to lend when others weren't.
- **Ordinary peasants did *not* use banks.** They used credit with neighbours, and pawned goods. Banks were for merchants, lords and kings, and **kings borrowing for wars and then not paying back** ruined several banks.
- **For our money rule:** a bank must only **move** money (deposits, loans, transfers), never create it. Loans are records of who owes whom.

### 2b. Neural networks and local AI for decisions *(owner's idea, being researched)*

Research: [`../research/2026-11-20-neural-agents.md`](../research/2026-11-20-neural-agents.md).

**What we learned:**
- **Small neural networks are cheap to run.** One per kind of agent (family, trader, lord), ~1,000 multiplications per decision: 200,000 families in well under a second. The hard part is **training** them, not running them.
- **Local language models are far too slow for the masses** (one round of 200,000 family decisions ≈ 110–170 hours), but fine for **a few notable characters' big decisions** (a lord deciding on war once a year).
- **Language models aren't repeatable** even at temperature 0, so their answers must be **recorded in the save** and replayed, to keep "same seed → same world".
- **Every working example is small** (tens to ~1,000 agents), and agents trained on a reward tend to **game the rules**.

**Proposed plan (to agree):**
1. **Scoring stays the default** and the source of truth. Built first.
2. **Neural "mode" as an experiment, later**, behind the same decision interface (same inputs, same choice of actions):
   - step 1, **imitation**: train a network to copy the scoring AI; check the world looks the same;
   - step 2, **neuroevolution**: each family's network weights are **inherited from its parents with small mutations**, like its personality traits; families that survive and prosper pass theirs on. The world "breeds" its own behaviour;
   - compare the two modes side by side.
3. **A local language model for a handful of notable people's big yearly decisions**, as an experiment, with every answer recorded. About 20 test decisions show whether it's worth it.
4. **Memories without neural networks:** a family keeps a short list of events (the famine of year 212, robbed on the forest road) that change its scores (fear of that road). Cheap, and explainable.

**Decided in the third round *(20 Nov)*:**
- **Two sides of the simulation:** a **scoring mode** (values and rules) and a **neural mode** (evolving networks). The **neural mode is the one the owner is most excited about.** Scoring is still built first, as the baseline to compare against.
- **Neuroevolution** is the favoured way to train: networks inherited and mutated across family generations.
- **"Understanding everything" means** understanding the maths, the logic and the ideas behind each system, **not** knowing why each network chose each action. Not knowing a network's "why" is accepted.
- **Training can take days** on the owner's PC, and **friends' PCs could help**: neuroevolution splits up naturally (each computer runs whole worlds and they swap the best networks). Free tools only (e.g. free online notebooks), in line with the no-paid-usage rule. Note: for neuroevolution the expensive part is running the simulation itself, so ordinary CPUs help as much as graphics cards.
- The "local AI only for words" decision is **softened**: never for the masses; maybe for a few notable people's big decisions, with every answer recorded.
- **A goal of the whole project:** see whether **real-life economics emerges** from the simulation, its successes and its problems (ideas in section 4).
- **Emergent behaviour from evolving networks is the owner's deepest interest**, beyond this project too: something to build over the next few years. Language models are less interesting to the owner than emergence.

### 2c. Case studies and a test project *(research, 20 Nov)*

Research: [`../research/2026-11-20-emergent-evolution.md`](../research/2026-11-20-emergent-evolution.md).

- **Closest examples:** *Polyworld* and *The Bibites* (evolving brains; predators, herding and species emerged from survival alone); DeepMind's **emergent bartering** (2022: agents learned to trade, price regions appeared, some agents became traders carrying goods between regions, but by reinforcement learning, not evolution); *Sugarscape* (trade and inequality from simple rules).
- **Nobody seems to have shown trade emerging from neuroevolution alone.** A real gap, so it's research, not a safe bet, and that's what makes it exciting.
- **Lessons:** fitness = survival and reproduction only; **the world must make specialisation pay** (goods in different places, a need for several, travel and carrying costs); keep diversity alive (competition, rivals, occasional disasters); thousands of generations; one world per CPU thread; always compare against a control (trade switched off); measure everything and chart it.
- **Proposed: a small test project first**, a "toy world" (64 × 64 grid, a few hundred agents, tiny networks), in three milestones: **survival** → **two resources in different places** → **exchange** (a "give" action). Proposed shape is in the conversation and the research note.

## 3. Groups and individuals

**Agreed:** everyone is stored as an individual record; **families decide** for their members; pops are totals. What's left is when someone becomes a **notable person** with their own goals and decisions.

### Becoming notable *(proposed triggers)*

- **Born into it:** heirs of lords, children of guild masters.
- **Rare talent:** high magic aptitude, a genius craftsman.
- **Doing something big:** getting rich, founding a village, leading a bandit band, leading people through a disaster, a famous journey, a bandit band going straight.
- **Taking a role:** trader, priest, guard captain, mayor, lord.
- **Going back:** a notable whose moment has passed (a retired trader) returns to deciding as part of their family, but keeps their name and story in the history.
- **A cap**, perhaps 10,000–50,000 notables at once, as Dwarf Fortress caps its historical figures, so it stays fast.

### Decided *(20 Nov)*

- **Every trader is tracked as an individual**, with their own price notebook. Traders are the heart of the game: the go-betweens between ordinary people and the wider economy, and they drive it.

## 4. The economy: emerging from the agents

**The aim** *(owner)*: the agents do everything, and the economy **emerges** from them. We design the small rules (how one family buys bread, how a baker sets a price); the big patterns (prices, booms, famines, routes) come out. We never write "bread costs 6".

**It has been done:** *Sugarscape* (Epstein and Axtell, 1996): simple agents gathering two kinds of "sugar" and trading; prices formed and converged, and inequality emerged, without being programmed. The Python prototype showed the other side: emergence needs solid foundations (money conserved, goods used up, enough production to feed everyone).

### How prices form: price beliefs *(agreed)*

From Doran and Parberry, simplified:
1. Every agent has a **belief** about what each good is worth, as a range (baker: bread 50–70 pennies).
2. On market day, sellers **ask** from their range, buyers **bid** from theirs.
3. If bid ≥ ask, they trade **halfway between**.
4. Everyone **learns**: a seller who sold easily nudges up; one who couldn't sell nudges down; a buyer who missed out nudges up.

| | Belief | Offer | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baker | 50–70 | asks 60 | |
| Family A | 40–60 | bids 50 | no trade; A raises its belief a little |
| Family B | 50–90 | bids 70 | **trade at 65**: 65 pennies move from B to the baker |

- **Beliefs are local** *(owner)*: they come from the prices an agent sees and hears about. Like the price of bread in the UK versus Japan: each place has its own idea of what things cost. A trader arriving from far away brings different beliefs.
- **Wages form the same way** *(agreed)*: families believe what their work is worth, employers what they'll pay.
- **A starting point**, to be upgraded with more advanced systems later.

### Market days *(agreed)*

- Towns trade on **weekly market days**; big **fairs** once a year. Hamlets barter any time.
- **The local lord (or town council) chooses the day**, for its own reasons. Neighbouring towns on different days means traders **move between them** rather than sitting in one place.
- *(History: a town needed a lord's or king's grant to hold a market.)*

### Money: minted by the dwarves *(owner's idea, agreed)*

- **Money is minted from mined silver and gold.** The amount of money can grow, so a "price revolution" (inflation after a big silver strike) can happen.
- **At the start, only dwarves mine the precious ore, and only dwarves mint coins.** The dwarves are rich because they make the currency, but **coin is also their main export**, and they must buy food. That should create interesting dynamics.
- Ideas to build on (suggestions, see the conversation):
  - **Minting profit** (*seigniorage*): a coin is worth more than its metal; the difference is the mint's income. Historically a big royal income.
  - **Trusted coin:** a clan whose coins are always pure becomes the standard, like the Florentine florin or Venetian ducat.
  - **Debasement:** a greedy dwarf king puts less silver in each coin → prices rise and trust falls (the Roman denarius; Henry VIII's "Great Debasement").
  - **Clan mint marks:** each clan's coins carry its mark, and trust can differ by clan.
  - **A geography of money:** coins flow outward from the dwarven mountains along trade routes, so money is plentiful near the dwarves and scarce far away, where people **barter** more. That would come out of the simulation by itself.
  - **The rich-but-dependent trap:** like Spain with American silver, a people that can make money may not develop other industries, and pays high food prices. Humans could refuse to sell food; dwarves could **stop minting** and cause a coin shortage (Europe had real "bullion famines" in the 1400s).
  - **Wear and loss:** coins wear out and get lost, so new minting is always needed. Allowed only as a deliberate, logged removal, never by accident.
  - **Later:** other races learn to mine and mint (knowledge layer), and counterfeiters appear.
- **The dwarves are the owner's favourite race:** expand their history, culture and magic later. Humans are the least exciting but essential (research); elves are cool for their magic.

### Track everything and show it *(owner)*

- **Every trade is recorded**: who, what, how much, what price, where, when.
- **Statistics viewers** for everything, above all trades, **prices of every good over time**, and **amounts of goods** as production, trade and consumption change them. The owner loves statistics.
- Practical note for later: a full world makes a lot of trades (hundreds of thousands a week), so we'll keep the full detail for a recent window and summaries (daily totals per town and good) forever.

### Real economics to watch for

| Pattern | What it is | Would show up as |
|---|---|---|
| One price, minus transport | prices in two towns differ by at most the cost of carrying goods between them | traders evening out prices on busy routes |
| Von Thünen's rings | gardens and dairy near a city, then firewood forest, grain, then herding | fields arranging themselves by distance from towns |
| Central places | market towns about a day's walk apart, fewer bigger cities above | a natural hierarchy of settlements |
| The hog cycle | high prices → everyone plants more → glut → crash | grain prices swinging year to year |
| The Malthusian trap | population grows until food runs short | villages growing until a bad harvest |
| Wages rise after a plague | fewer workers, each worth more (after the Black Death) | survivors getting richer |
| The price revolution | new silver → more coins → higher prices | inflation after a dwarven silver strike |
| Engel's law | richer families spend a smaller share on food | rich towns buying luxuries |

**Decided at the end of the session *(20 Nov)*:** the owner likes **all** the dwarven-money ideas above (minting profit, trusted coin and clan marks, debasement, the geography of money, rich-but-dependent politics, wear and loss, others learning to mint later, counterfeiters).
