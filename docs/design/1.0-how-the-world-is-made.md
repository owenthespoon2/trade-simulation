# 1.0 How the world is made

*Status: **understood** (agreed 20 November 2026). Started 20 November 2026.*

This note is the map for all of Stage 1. Each step gets its own detailed note later (1.1 height, 1.2 temperature, and so on). Here we only fix the **order**, the **shape** of each step, the **size and units** of the world, and how we'll **check** it.

---

## 1. What it does

**A world generator.** You give it one number, the **seed**, and it produces the whole world: the height of the land, the temperature, the rain, the rivers and lakes, how fertile the soil is, and the biome at every point. The same seed always gives exactly the same world. A different seed gives a different world.

This is how Dwarf Fortress does it too: first the land, then the climate, then the water, then what grows. Each step only uses the steps before it.

## 2. The pipeline

```
 seed
  │
  ▼
 1.1 HEIGHT ─────────► land and sea, mountains, hills, plains
  │
  ├──► 1.2 TEMPERATURE   from how far north, and how high
  │
  ├──► 1.3 RAIN          wind carries sea moisture inland; mountains wring it out
  │
  ▼
 1.4 RIVERS AND LAKES    rain flows downhill and collects
  │
  ▼
 1.5 FERTILITY + BIOMES  what grows where
  │
  ▼
 1.6 MAP VIEWER          a picture of every layer
```

| Step | Reads | Produces | The core idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1.1 Height** | seed | height in metres at every point | Noise (the September lessons) shaped so there's ocean round the edge and mountain ranges inland |
| **1.2 Temperature** | height | average yearly temperature, °C | Colder to the north, and **6.5 °C colder per 1,000 m up** (the real-world average "lapse rate") |
| **1.3 Rain** | height | yearly rainfall, mm | Wind blows in from the sea carrying moisture. It rains more where the air is pushed uphill, and the far side of a mountain range is dry: a **rain shadow** |
| **1.4 Rivers and lakes** | height, rain | where water flows, how much, where it pools | Each point drains to its lowest neighbour. Add up all the rain upstream of a point: where that total is big, there's a river. Dips fill up into lakes until they overflow |
| **1.5 Fertility and biomes** | temperature, rain, rivers, slope | soil fertility 0–1, and a biome at every point | A lookup table of **temperature × rain → biome** (the Whittaker diagram, below), plus special cases for water, mountains and marshes |
| **1.6 Map viewer** | all of the above | pictures | One image per layer, so we can see whether each step looks right |

**Weather comes later.** These steps make the **climate**: the long-term average. **Weather** is the day-to-day and year-to-year variation around that average (a wet spring, a drought year). It sits on top of the climate in stage 8, so it doesn't belong in world generation.

## 3. An important change from September: formula *and* grid

In September we decided **"the land is a formula"**: height at any point is calculated, never stored. That still works for **height**, because the height at a point only depends on that point.

**Rivers and rain don't work like that.**
- Whether a point has a river depends on all the land **uphill** of it, possibly hundreds of km away.
- Whether a point gets rain depends on all the mountains **upwind** of it.

These can't be calculated one point at a time; they need the whole map at once. So:

> **Height stays a formula. At world creation, we sample it onto a grid, and climate and rivers are calculated once on that grid and stored.**

| Layer | How it's kept | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Height | Formula, any detail, plus a stored grid copy | Detail for close-up views, a grid for everything that needs neighbours |
| Temperature | Could be a formula, stored on the grid for speed | It only needs height and position |
| Rain, rivers, lakes | Stored on the grid | They need the whole map |
| Fertility, biomes | Stored on the grid | They come from the layers above |
| Changes made by people (fields, roads) | Stored only where they happen | As decided in September |

**Proposed grid: 1 cell = 1 km × 1 km.** Fine enough for rivers and towns, and coarse enough to be quick.

## 4. Size and units

### Units

| Quantity | Unit |
|---|---|
| Distance | km (x runs west → east, y runs south → north) |
| Height | metres above sea level; below 0 is sea |
| Temperature | °C, average over the year |
| Rain | mm per year |
| Fertility | 0 (nothing grows) to 1 (best soil) |
| Time | 1 tick = 1 day for now (the time-scale problem is still open) |

### The final world: proposed 1,024 × 1,024 km

- That's about **1 million km²**, with roughly 40% land, so about **400,000 km² of land: about Japan's size** (378,000 km²).
- At 1 km per cell, that's **1,048,576 cells**. Storing ten layers of 4-byte numbers is about **40 MB**: easy for a PC.
- 1,024 is a power of two (2¹⁰), which makes halving the grid for zoomed-out views neat.

### The test region: proposed 256 × 256 km

- **65,536 cells**, so each run takes a fraction of a second and we can try many seeds while tuning.
- Big enough for a mountain range, a coastline, a few rivers and a handful of biomes.
- **One cheat:** in reality, 256 km north to south only changes the temperature by about 2–3 °C, which is too little to see different climates. So the test region gets a **squashed climate**: it runs from cold in the north to warm in the south, as if it were a whole country. When we scale up to 1,024 km, the climate un-squashes.

### Climate range

| Where | Average yearly temperature at sea level |
|---|---|
| Far north | about −2 °C (like northern Hokkaido or southern Finland) |
| Middle | about 10 °C |
| Far south | about 22 °C (like Okinawa) |

Mountains are colder still, at 6.5 °C per 1,000 m. The **prevailing wind comes from the west**, so the west coasts are wet and the land east of the mountains is drier.

## 5. One seed, many seeds

The world has one seed. Each step makes its own seed from it, for example `heightSeed = hash(worldSeed, 1)` and `rainSeed = hash(worldSeed, 3)`, using the same hash as in the September notes. **Why:** if we change how rain works, the mountains stay exactly where they were, so we can compare before and after.

## 6. Biomes: the Whittaker diagram

Ecologists found that **average temperature and yearly rain** decide most of what grows. Our first version of the table (each cell is a biome):

| | **< 250 mm** | **250–600 mm** | **600–1,500 mm** | **> 1,500 mm** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **below −5 °C** | ice | tundra | tundra | tundra |
| **−5 to 5 °C** | cold desert | cold steppe | taiga (pine forest) | taiga |
| **5 to 18 °C** | desert | grassland | temperate forest | temperate rainforest |
| **above 18 °C** | hot desert | savanna | dry tropical forest | **bamboo and subtropical forest** |

**Special cases come first:**
1. below sea level → **ocean**; in a filled dip → **lake**;
2. on a strong river → **river**;
3. very high or very steep → **mountain** (bare rock, or **snow** if it's below −5 °C);
4. flat, wet and next to water → **marsh**;
5. a thin strip at the sea's edge → **beach**.

The numbers in the table will be tuned in note 1.5. This is just the shape.

## 7. Worked example: two points through the pipeline

Final world, 1,024 km. The sea-level temperature drops evenly from 22 °C at the south edge (y = 0) to −2 °C at the north edge (y = 1,024):

```
seaLevelTemp(y) = 22 − 24 × (y / 1024)
temperature     = seaLevelTemp(y) − 6.5 × (height / 1000)
```

**Point A, a hillside in the middle of the country.** y = 512, height 600 m, rain (from step 1.3) 1,200 mm.

| Step | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Sea-level temperature | 22 − 24 × (512 / 1024) = 22 − 12 | 10 °C |
| Temperature | 10 − 6.5 × 0.6 = 10 − 3.9 | **6.1 °C** |
| Biome | 5–18 °C row, 600–1,500 mm column | **temperate forest** |

**Point B, a southern lowland.** y = 100, height 50 m, rain 1,800 mm.

| Step | Working | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Sea-level temperature | 22 − 24 × (100 / 1024) = 22 − 2.34 | 19.66 °C |
| Temperature | 19.66 − 6.5 × 0.05 = 19.66 − 0.33 | **19.3 °C** |
| Biome | above 18 °C row, > 1,500 mm column | **bamboo and subtropical forest**: tea country |

These become the first tests: the code must give exactly these numbers.

## 8. How it'll be built and checked

- **C#:**
  - one library project holds the world generator;
  - one small program runs it and saves **a PNG picture per layer** (height, temperature, rain, rivers, biomes);
  - one test project checks the worked examples.
- **Checking each step:**
  - look at its picture;
  - check that a few points match hand calculations;
  - run it with five seeds to make sure nothing silly happens: no rivers flowing uphill, no snow on the south coast, no desert next to a lake.

## 9. What's simplified, for now

- **The temperature model** is just "north is cold, and high is cold". No ocean currents, and no warm coasts versus cold interiors.
- **The wind blows from one direction** everywhere. Real winds change with latitude and season.
- **The rivers are one-cell-wide paths** on a 1 km grid. Close up, they'll need smoothing so they don't look blocky.
- **There's no erosion.** Real mountains are shaped by rivers wearing them down over millions of years, which gives realistic valleys. It's a possible upgrade later, and an expensive one.
- **The rock type** (good for mines? good soil?) is left out for now. It's a likely addition for dwarves and fertility.

## 11. Questions and answers

**20 November 2026:** all five proposals agreed as written:

1. Height stays a formula; climate, rivers, fertility and biomes are calculated once on a 1 km grid and stored.
2. Final world 1,024 × 1,024 km; test region 256 × 256 km.
3. Cold north (about −2 °C), warm south (about 22 °C), wind from the west.
4. The test region uses a squashed climate for now.
5. Nothing missing for now; weather, erosion and rock types stay as later upgrades.

**Open point (20 Nov), for Stage 2:** if the realistic generator adds **erosion**, rivers will reshape the land, so the eroded height has to be **stored on the 1 km grid** rather than recalculated from the formula. Noise can still add detail finer than 1 km on top. To be decided in Stage 2's design note.
